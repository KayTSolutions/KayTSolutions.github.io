Time tracking that runs itself, for a game studio of 18

Denda Games planned everything in Trello but had no idea how long anything actually took. I built a system that turns the boards they already used into daily reports for management.

Client
Denda Games, Hengelo
My role
Project management intern, now freelance through KTS
When
February 2026 until now
Tools
Trello, Make, Google Sheets, Apps Script, Notion

The problem

Denda Games is a studio that ports games to new platforms and builds web games for clients. My brief was broad: improve how the team works. When I looked closer, one thing was missing completely. There was no time tracking, so estimates for new projects were based on gut feeling.

Asking 18 busy people to start and stop timers was never going to last. Whatever I built had to work with what they already did every day: moving cards on Trello boards.

How it works

Moving a card is the only thing anyone has to do. Everything after that happens on its own.

  1. TrelloThe team moves cards as usual. Six boards are tracked.
  2. MakeScenarios log every card that enters or leaves “In progress”, with who and when.
  3. Google SheetsSessions become hours, checked against each person’s working hours, holidays and leave.
  4. Apps ScriptAfter every working day, a script builds a report per board and per person.
  5. EmailManagement gets the report, plus a morning email about who is absent.

What the report shows

  • What each person worked on and for how long
  • Gaps of more than 15 minutes where someone had no task in progress
  • Tasks that are still open at the end of the day
  • Estimated hours compared with the hours actually counted, so over- and underestimates stand out

The real data and reports are confidential under my contract, so what you see here is the structure and sample data, not Denda’s numbers.

Beyond the numbers

Data only helps if people can find it. In the second half of my internship I also rebuilt Denda’s Notion workspace: a cleaner onboarding page, a landing page for future interns, and a dashboard for employees where sensitive project details stay hidden from guests.

I presented my findings and proposals to management along the way and explained each workflow change to the team, so the system was something people understood, not something done to them.

Result

Denda now has real data behind its estimates, and it’s used every day through the reports, not just stored. When my internship ended, the company asked me to stay on. I registered my own company, KTS (KayTSolutions), in July 2026, and Denda is still my client: I make changes and improvements whenever they ask.

What I learned: a vague brief is an opportunity. Asking the right questions and building something small that works teaches you more than waiting for a perfect plan.
